This page is a case, not a recipe written in advance. On 2026-09-25 the path
below was run end to end in the family lab, on two virtual machines, and every
screen, command and output on this page is what that run captured. The TUIs ran
inside tmux and each pane was saved with `tmux capture-pane`; to fit the page,
the dialog frames are removed and blank lines dropped, and nothing else is
changed. Secrets are shown as placeholders such as `<ALICE_PASSWORD>`: in the
run they travelled on standard input or through a tmux paste buffer, never on
a command line.

The versions: [tui-cert v0.3.0](https://github.com/tui-tools/tui-cert/releases/tag/v0.3.0)
for the CA and Keycloak's certificate, [v0.3.1](https://github.com/tui-tools/tui-cert/releases/tag/v0.3.1)
for Headscale's (the repository moved on between the two halves of the run),
[tui-tailscale v1.0.0](https://github.com/tui-tools/tui-tailscale/releases/tag/v1.0.0),
[tui-firewall v0.5.0](https://github.com/tui-tools/tui-firewall/releases/tag/v0.5.0),
Headscale 0.29.3 from pkgs.tui.tools, Tailscale 1.102.4 and Keycloak 26.7.4.
The control plane was Ubuntu 24.04; the laptop was Omarchy Server 4.0.1, an
Arch-based system.

The run also found nine problems in the tools, and each one is shown where it
happened. They are collected, with links, in [what we found](#what-we-found).

## What you end up with

A small company wants its own tailnet. People join from their laptops by
signing in with their company account, and only the people the company put in
one group get in. Nobody hands out keys by hand for laptops, nothing depends on
a public certificate authority, and nothing anywhere skips TLS verification.

The pieces, with the names the lab used:

<div class="table-wrap">

| Piece | Where | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CA `lab-ca` | control plane | a local certificate authority kept by [tui-cert](/tools/tui-cert/); every machine that takes part trusts it |
| Keycloak | `https://sso.lab.internal:18443` | the identity provider, realm `company`, served with a certificate from `lab-ca` |
| Headscale | `https://vpn.lab.internal:19443` | the tailnet's control plane, driven by [tui-tailscale](/tools/tui-tailscale/), with its own certificate from `lab-ca` |
| Group `vpn-users` | in Keycloak | the one allow list: `alice` is a member, `bob` is not |
| `company.internal` | MagicDNS | the names nodes get, such as `laptop.company.internal` |

</div>

The control plane is also the first node, and it routes a subnet behind it to
everyone who accepts routes. At the end, alice's laptop is on the tailnet and
reaches that subnet, and bob, who has a perfectly good Keycloak account, is
turned away at the browser.

## How the lab differs from a real network

Read this before you copy a value, because four things here exist only because
it is a lab.

- Both machines are QEMU guests behind their own user-mode NAT. They cannot
  reach each other directly. Both reach the lab host's loopback as `10.0.2.2`,
  the QEMU gateway, so the host forwards `127.0.0.1:18443` and
  `127.0.0.1:19443` into the control plane over ssh, and every guest sees
  Keycloak and Headscale at `10.0.2.2`. That is also why the ports are 18443
  and 19443 and not 443.
- The names `sso.lab.internal` and `vpn.lab.internal` live in `/etc/hosts` on
  both guests, pointing at `10.0.2.2`. On a real network they are records in
  your internal DNS, and the certificates carry whatever address those
  records point at.
- UDP does not cross an ssh forward, so the two nodes never talk directly.
  Their traffic went through a DERP relay, and, as the run showed, the relay
  was one of Tailscale's public servers. On a real network with `41641/udp`
  open, nodes connect directly and the relay is a fallback.
- The person at the laptop is a headless Chromium driven by a short Playwright
  script, `kc-login.py`, that fills in Keycloak's form and prints where the
  browser landed. It uses the system Chromium with the system trust store and
  `ignore_https_errors` false, so the browser verifies every certificate
  exactly as a person's would. On a real laptop, a person opens the same URL
  and types the same password.

Also, because the traffic reaches the control plane through a forward that
ends on its loopback, the host firewall was never in the path. The firewall
rules below are the ones a real host needs; in this lab they changed nothing
that could be measured.

## Part one: the CA and Keycloak

### 1. The CA (tui-cert, `5`, then `N`)

On the control plane, after the one-time [repository setup](/install/),
`sudo apt-get install -y tui-cert`. In tui-cert, screen `5` lists local CAs and
`N` creates one. The name was set to `lab-ca`; the key (ECDSA P-256) and the
validity (ten years) stayed at their defaults. The confirm dialog:

```text
Create the CA lab-ca

This writes:
  /etc/tui-cert/ca/lab-ca/ca.crt (0644)
  /etc/tui-cert/ca/lab-ca/ca.key (0600, never shown)

Subject /CN=lab-ca, valid 3650 days, basicConstraints CA:TRUE, pathlen:0

Whoever holds /etc/tui-cert/ca/lab-ca/ca.key can sign a certificate for any name, and every machine that trusts this CA will
accept it. The key stays on this machine at mode 600 and is never shown; back it up somewhere only root can read, or accept that
losing it means re-issuing everything and re-trusting a new CA everywhere.

Command to run:
$ /usr/bin/sudo -n install -d -m 755 /etc/tui-cert/ca/lab-ca
$ /usr/bin/sudo -n openssl req -x509 -newkey ec -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:prime256v1 -nodes -keyout
  /etc/tui-cert/ca/lab-ca/ca.key -out /etc/tui-cert/ca/lab-ca/ca.crt -days 3650 -set_serial 0x3b8857bcbca3458a9041a03a98c6d62f
  -subj /CN=lab-ca -addext basicConstraints=critical,CA:TRUE,pathlen:0 -addext keyUsage=critical,keyCertSign,cRLSign
$ /usr/bin/sudo -n chmod 600 /etc/tui-cert/ca/lab-ca/ca.key
$ /usr/bin/sudo -n chmod 644 /etc/tui-cert/ca/lab-ca/ca.crt

y confirm    n cancel
```

After `y`, the CA screen:

### 2. Keycloak's certificate (`e`)

With `lab-ca` selected, `e` issues a server certificate: common name
`sso.lab.internal`, other names `10.0.2.2`, key and validity at their defaults
(397 days). The owner needs a word. Keycloak runs in a rootful podman container
as uid 1000, and the key is written at mode 600, so the container can read it
only if uid 1000 owns it. On this guest uid 1000 is the `lab` account, so the
owner was `lab:lab`. tui-cert 0.3.0 accepts only account names here; a numeric
`uid:gid` for a container is one of the things the run asked for.

```text
Issue sso.lab.internal from lab-ca

This writes:
  /etc/tui-cert/issued/sso.lab.internal/fullchain.pem (0644: the certificate, then lab-ca's)
  /etc/tui-cert/issued/sso.lab.internal/privkey.pem (0600, never shown)

Owned by lab:lab. Subject /CN=sso.lab.internal
subjectAltName=DNS:sso.lab.internal,IP:10.0.2.2

lab-ca is not in this machine's trust store, so clients here will refuse the certificate until it is (t on screen 5). Every other
client that connects has to trust lab-ca too.

Command to run:
$ /usr/bin/sudo -n install -d -m 755 /etc/tui-cert/issued/sso.lab.internal
$ /usr/bin/sudo -n openssl req -x509 -CA /etc/tui-cert/ca/lab-ca/ca.crt -CAkey /etc/tui-cert/ca/lab-ca/ca.key -newkey ec -pkeyopt
  ec_paramgen_curve:prime256v1 -nodes -keyout /etc/tui-cert/issued/sso.lab.internal/privkey.pem -out
  /etc/tui-cert/issued/sso.lab.internal/fullchain.pem -days 397 -set_serial 0x7e42db509e2c8cd3a7f6a1477bec35df -subj
  /CN=sso.lab.internal -addext subjectAltName=DNS:sso.lab.internal,IP:10.0.2.2 -addext basicConstraints=critical,CA:FALSE -addext
  keyUsage=critical,digitalSignature -addext extendedKeyUsage=serverAuth
$ /usr/bin/sudo -n tee -a /etc/tui-cert/issued/sso.lab.internal/fullchain.pem
$ /usr/bin/sudo -n chmod 600 /etc/tui-cert/issued/sso.lab.internal/privkey.pem
$ /usr/bin/sudo -n chmod 644 /etc/tui-cert/issued/sso.lab.internal/fullchain.pem
$ /usr/bin/sudo -n chown lab:lab /etc/tui-cert/issued/sso.lab.internal/privkey.pem
  /etc/tui-cert/issued/sso.lab.internal/fullchain.pem

y confirm    n cancel
```

The dialog already says the next step: this machine does not trust `lab-ca`
yet.

### 4. Keycloak as a systemd quadlet

Keycloak is not a family tool; it runs in podman, and systemd runs podman
through a quadlet, a unit file that describes a container. This is the one
the run used, `/etc/containers/systemd/keycloak.container`:

```ini
# Keycloak for the company tailnet case, run by podman through systemd (quadlet).
[Unit]
Description=Keycloak (realm company)
After=network-online.target
Wants=network-online.target

[Container]
ContainerName=keycloak
Image=quay.io/keycloak/keycloak:26.7.4
Exec=start --db=dev-file --hostname=https://sso.lab.internal:18443 --https-port=18443 --http-enabled=false --https-certificate-file=/etc/kc-tls/fullchain.pem --https-certificate-key-file=/etc/kc-tls/privkey.pem --health-enabled=true
PublishPort=18443:18443
Volume=/etc/tui-cert/issued/sso.lab.internal:/etc/kc-tls:ro
Volume=keycloak-data:/opt/keycloak/data
EnvironmentFile=/etc/keycloak/admin.env

[Service]
Restart=always
TimeoutStartSec=300

[Install]
WantedBy=multi-user.target default.target
```

The pair tui-cert issued is mounted read-only, http is off, and `--hostname`
is the full external URL, so the issuer Keycloak advertises is the same URL
the browser, Headscale and curl all use. `dev-file` is Keycloak's embedded
database, fine for a lab and not for production: give a real deployment a real
database. The first start ran without `--db` and Keycloak warned that relying
on the default database in production mode is deprecated, so the option was
added and the unit restarted. The bootstrap admin's name and password are in
`/etc/keycloak/admin.env`, mode 600.

```console
lab@ubuntu$ sudo install -d -m 700 /etc/keycloak
lab@ubuntu$ sudo install -m 600 /tmp/admin.env /etc/keycloak/admin.env   # KC_BOOTSTRAP_ADMIN_USERNAME=admin, KC_BOOTSTRAP_ADMIN_PASSWORD=<ADMIN_PASSWORD>
lab@ubuntu$ sudo install -m 644 /tmp/keycloak.container /etc/containers/systemd/keycloak.container
lab@ubuntu$ echo "10.0.2.2 sso.lab.internal vpn.lab.internal" | sudo tee -a /etc/hosts
lab@ubuntu$ sudo systemctl daemon-reload && sudo systemctl start keycloak
lab@ubuntu$ systemctl status keycloak --no-pager | head -3
● keycloak.service - Keycloak (realm company)
     Loaded: loaded (/etc/containers/systemd/keycloak.container; generated)
     Active: active (running) since Fri 2026-09-25 15:46:46 UTC
```

Then, from the control plane, through the lab's forward, verified against the

### 5. The realm, the client and the group

The realm was built with Keycloak's admin REST API and curl, from a short
script; the admin console does the same thing with forms. What it created:

- realm `company`;
- group `vpn-users`;
- user `alice`, a member of `vpn-users`, and user `bob`, in no group, both
  with a password, a verified email at `company.example` and a full name;
- client `headscale`: confidential (it has a client secret), standard flow
  (the authorization-code flow a browser login uses), the redirect URI
  `https://vpn.lab.internal:19443/oidc/callback`, and one protocol mapper.

The mapper is the piece everything else depends on. Keycloak puts group
membership in a token only when a mapper adds it, and Headscale can only check
`allowed_groups` against a `groups` claim that is there. This is the client
definition from the script (`$VPN_CALLBACK` is the redirect URI above):

```json
{
  "clientId": "headscale",
  "name": "headscale",
  "protocol": "openid-connect",
  "publicClient": false,
  "clientAuthenticatorType": "client-secret",
  "standardFlowEnabled": true,
  "directAccessGrantsEnabled": true,
  "redirectUris": ["$VPN_CALLBACK"],
  "webOrigins": ["+"],
  "protocolMappers": [{
    "name": "groups",
    "protocol": "openid-connect",
    "protocolMapper": "oidc-group-membership-mapper",
    "config": {
      "claim.name": "groups",
      "full.path": "false",
      "id.token.claim": "true",
      "access.token.claim": "true",
      "userinfo.token.claim": "true"
    }
  }]
}
```

`full.path` false makes the claim read `vpn-users` rather than `/vpn-users`,
which is the value `allowed_groups` will be given. What the script printed:


The laptop's browser is going to meet both Keycloak and Headscale, so the
laptop has to trust `lab-ca` before anything else. On the laptop, the same
repository setup and `sudo pacman -S --noconfirm tui-cert`. On the control plane, `x` on

## What we found

Nine issues were filed from this run. All of them are open at the time of
writing; the tui-tailscale ones are being fixed for tui-tailscale 1.0.1, and
the tui-kit ones for the next tui-kit release, which every tool picks up. None
of them is claimed fixed here.

- [tui-tailscale#23](https://github.com/tui-tools/tui-tailscale/issues/23):
  installing headscale or tailscale with `i` hangs on Ubuntu, because
  needrestart waits on debconf after `apt-get install` (step 7, and again in
  step 12).
- [tui-tailscale#24](https://github.com/tui-tools/tui-tailscale/issues/24):
  the readiness line's nftables reader reports ports closed on a ufw host
  whose ports are open (step 11).
- [tui-tailscale#25](https://github.com/tui-tools/tui-tailscale/issues/25):
  tui-firewall installed while tui-tailscale runs is still treated as missing
  by `f` and by the readiness reader (step 11).
- [tui-tailscale#26](https://github.com/tui-tools/tui-tailscale/issues/26):
  the nodes screen offers `R` for a registration the identity provider's
  policy just refused (step 14).
- [tui-tailscale#27](https://github.com/tui-tools/tui-tailscale/issues/27):
  a private tailnet still relays through Tailscale's public DERP servers, and
  nothing says so (step 13).
- [tui-kit#30](https://github.com/tui-tools/tui-kit/issues/30): the confirm
  dialog collapses spaces inside diff lines, so YAML indentation disappears
  from previews (steps 9 and 10).
- [tui-kit#31](https://github.com/tui-tools/tui-kit/issues/31): command
  previews are not shell-quoted, so an argument with spaces or shell syntax
  reads as a different command (steps 9, 10 and 11).
- [tui-cert#24](https://github.com/tui-tools/tui-cert/issues/24): the local
  CA in practice: `x` assumes ssh by bare host name, `t` on Ubuntu reports the
  first line of `update-ca-certificates` as its result, the owner field needs
  numeric ids for containerised services, and prefilled fields need a quick
  way to clear them (steps 2, 3 and 6).
- tui-tools/pkgs#13, in the family's packaging repository, which is private:
  the headscale package reports its version as `v0.29.3+dirty` (step 9).

What the run proves: a Headscale control plane configured entirely through
tui-tailscale, with a certificate from a tui-cert CA, admitted a Keycloak
user in the allowed group through a real browser login with verification on,
routed a subnet to her, and refused a Keycloak user outside the group. What
it does not prove: behaviour on a network where the nodes can reach each
other over UDP, names served by real DNS, ports reached through the host
firewall rather than around it, or any identity provider but Keycloak 26.7.4.
